Jet engines run more efficiently when the materials inside them tolerate more heat with less cooling. Ceramic matrix composites already do that job in commercial engines, made by conventional routes. Ceramic matrix composite 3D printing for aerospace turbines is a research effort to add the design freedom of additive manufacturing, and the published results show both the appeal and the distance still to cover.

## What Are Ceramic Matrix Composites?

A ceramic matrix composite (CMC) consists of ceramic fibers embedded in a ceramic matrix. GE makes its turbine CMCs from silicon carbide (SiC) fibers embedded in a silicon carbide matrix. The fiber reinforcement addresses the brittleness of ceramics: fibers bridge cracks that form in the matrix, which depends on a weak bond between fibers and matrix so that fibers can pull out rather than break with it.

Compared with the nickel-based alloys used in turbine hot sections, the published claims are:

- Density: An American Ceramic Society Bulletin article on GE Aviation's CMC program states that the material density of CMCs is one-third that of today's nickel-based alloys, and link this to a turbine component weight reduction of over 50 percent.
- Temperature: GE says its CMCs can operate at 2,400 degrees Fahrenheit, 500 degrees higher than the most advanced alloys.
- Cooling: because the material tolerates more heat, less air has to be diverted from the engine flow path to cool hot-section components, and minimizing cooling flow increases the overall efficiency of the cycle.

## How CMCs Are Made Today

Conventional CMC routes include chemical vapor infiltration (CVI), polymer infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP), and infiltration with molten silicon. In CVI, reactive gases deposit matrix material inside a fibrous preform at elevated temperature; the processing time is typically very long and the residual porosity is about 10 to 15%. PIP usually takes five to eight infiltration cycles. GE's production material is densified by molten silicon infiltration.

## CMCs Already Flying

The turbine shroud was the first CMC turbine component GE manufactured widely. The CMC shroud in the LEAP engine was certified by the FAA and EASA in May 2016, and by 2019 GE reported having made more than 40,000 CMC turbine shrouds. The GE9X design goes further, with a CMC combustor inner liner and outer liner, HPT Stage 1 shrouds and nozzles, and HPT Stage 2 nozzles. These parts are made by conventional CMC processing, not by 3D printing.

## What Has Been Shown for 3D Printing

NASA Glenn Research Center has published the most directly relevant work. In a 2016 conference presentation, its researchers noted that additive manufacturing had been demonstrated for metals and polymer matrix composites but that work on ceramic matrix composites had been limited. They described several approaches for SiC-based materials.

### Binder Jetting

Binder jetting deposits a liquid binder into a powder bed. In a NASA project titled "A Fully Nonmetallic Gas Turbine Engine Enabled by Additive Manufacturing," the team binder-jetted SiC powders with median particle sizes from 9.3 to 53.0 microns, then infiltrated the printed parts with polycarbosilane, phenolic resin or liquid silicon. SiC fibers were added to the powder bed to form CMC materials.

The results were mixed. Samples from the fiber-containing panels had higher strengths and strains to failure than unreinforced ones, with the best set averaging 66 MPa in four-point bending. But the fracture surfaces showed no fiber pullout, and the authors concluded that the material did not exhibit true composite behavior. Additional infiltration steps were needed for densification, reaching maximum densities of 2.10 to 2.15 g/cc. The team did use the method to fabricate two turbine engine vane components of different designs and sizes, and noted that binder jetting could make prototype and production parts quickly and economically without tooling or extensive hand layup.

### Laminated Object Manufacturing

In laminated object manufacturing, fiber prepreg laminates are laser-cut to shape, stacked into the desired part, and then given high-temperature heat treatments. NASA presented this as a second route to additively manufactured CMCs.

### Paste Extrusion

NASA also reported using low-cost 3D printers with pre-ceramic pastes that convert to SiC after firing. This is related to robocasting, or direct ink writing, in which a paste is extruded through a small nozzle layer by layer and drying, debinding and sintering usually follow.

## Where Development Stands

On the published evidence, 3D-printed CMCs are at the research stage. Conventionally made SiC/SiC parts are certified and flying, while the NASA binder-jet work produced demonstration vanes and test panels that did not yet behave as true composites. The motivation remains what it was: SiC-based CMCs can reduce or eliminate the cooling air needed by shrouds, combustor liners, vanes and blades, and additive manufacturing offers part geometry without tooling or hand layup.
